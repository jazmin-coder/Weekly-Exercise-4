Introduction
Civic technology is a field that leverages technology and data to promote civic engagement
and public participation.  When e-government was first introduced in the 1990s, civic tech
focused on helping government employees improve their processes. However, it has since evolved
into a powerful tool to make government more responsive and efficient for the public,
while creatively addressing social issues.
Essentially, civic tech empowers individuals to use their skills for innovation and
engage in the democratic process by providing access to government resources. In 2009, 
President Obama signed the Memorandum on Transparency and Open Government, which resulted 
in the creation of Data.gov, the government's open data portal (Obama). Open data portals
enable citizens to initiate projects that governments may not be able to tackle on their own.
For instance, applications like Citizen demonstrate how communities can utilize 
public data to send real-time safety alerts and coordinate responses to emergencies 
such as natural disasters or protests. With access to open data, citizens can collaborate 
with the government rather than passively receive services.
Tim O'Reilly introduced the idea of "Government 2.0," which shows how civic technology
changes the way citizens interact with government. Instead of just paying taxes and
receiving services like a vending machine, citizens can now play a more active role
in shaping government actions and decisions.
Civic technology is also key to making smart cities work better. Smart cities use 
civic tech to collect and analyze data from sensors and open data portals. This helps
improve how cities operate, manage resources, and provide public services. By turning 
data into useful tools, civic tech allows city leaders and residents to work together 
to find sustainable solutions. Smart cities focus on data-driven governance that responds
to community needs rather than relying solely on top-down decisions.
Despite its potential, implementing civic tech solutions faces significant challenges.
A key issue is whether reliable data is available. According to the 14 Principles
of Open Data, specific criteria must be met to ensure that open data is accessible
and valuable to the public. Tauberer notes that open data should be “free, comprehensive,
original, timely, easy to access, machine-readable, fair, based on open standards, 
license-free, permanent, reusable, detailed, and trustworthy, while also allowing for
public input” (Tauberer). Local governments can enact laws to support data release based
on these principles and establish open data portals to provide public datasets, thereby
enhancing transparency.
Civic tech initiatives can sometimes become tied to politics, reducing their effectiveness.
To avoid this, it is vital to focus on the community's needs and involve many
stakeholders in decision-making. Collaborating with public servants who specialize in
public policy is critical, as they can share important insights into the regulations
that guide actions (Harrell).
